Record watch: transcription and justice in Canada and the U.S. — week of October 12, 2026

Oklahoma’s Supreme Court launches courtroom recording but keeps the official transcript with certified reporters; in California, court interpreters walk out over pay and remote interpreting.

October 19, 2026 · Watch · 3 min read

§ 1

Oklahoma: the Supreme Court records courtrooms, and keeps the court reporter

On October 12, 2026, the Supreme Court of Oklahoma authorized by order a three-year pilot: speech-to-text recording of proceedings, with secure cloud storage, in designated district courtrooms. A week earlier, state lawmakers were studying the same idea (our mid-October watch). The Administrative Director of the Courts will choose the locations, with the Chief Justice’s approval, giving priority to districts that struggle to find certified court reporters; the presiding judge decides, case by case, whether the technology is appropriate. The rules are clear: only a certified court reporter may prepare and certify an official transcript; recordings and the machine’s preliminary transcripts remain judicial work product, unavailable through open records requests or subpoenas; and they may not be used to train or improve artificial intelligence models. In dissent, Justice Combs faulted the Court for adopting the rules without input from the court reporter task force created in 2017. The Court called for a plan for counties with reporter vacancies; it has not ordered statewide implementation.

Why it matters: the machine records and offers a draft, but the official record remains the transcript a certified person prepared and signed.

§ 2

California: court interpreters walk out

On October 16, 2026, staff interpreters at the Region 2 courts of California, on the state’s northern coast, held a half-day walkout. In San Francisco, they picketed outside the courthouse at 350 McAllister Street, then outside the Supreme Court of California building, where the state’s Judicial Council was meeting. Their union, the California Alliance of Legal Interpreters, had announced on October 12 that 96% of members had authorized a strike, after more than eight months of bargaining; it represents 91 full-time interpreters in 12 counties. It is asking for pay comparable to federal court interpreters, who earn 35 to 47% more by its count, and for limits on remote interpreting: only for simple, non-evidentiary hearings, with an interpreter in the room whenever the person is. According to the union, the full-time interpreter workforce has fallen 42% in nine years, and the courts rely more and more on contractors. The region’s courts say they are committed to a fair agreement, have proposed mediation, and say essential language services continue; in San Francisco, some cases could be delayed.

Why it matters: in U.S. practice, the record keeps only the English interpretation (see our FAQ); when the interpreter is missing or remote, the witness’s own words survive only on the recording.

§ 3

What we take from it

Two states, one answer. In Oklahoma, the machine records, but only a certified reporter signs the official transcript; in California, interpreters ask to be in the room when the evidence is given. Either way, what goes into the record depends on a person who heard it and answers for it. That is how we work: we start from the recording, a person listens to it, transcribes each language as it was spoken, and signs the declaration of accuracy.
